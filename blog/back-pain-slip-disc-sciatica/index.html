Back pain, slip disc, and sciatica: what is actually going on, and what helps

Nearly half of all Indian adults will deal with lower back pain at some point. Many of them will hear the words "slip disc" or "sciatica" and immediately think surgery. The reality is more complicated — and, for most people, more encouraging than that.

Spine & Back Pain

If you have been told you have a "slip disc," the first thing worth knowing is that discs do not actually slip. They do not slide out of place like a coin from a stack. What happens is that the tough outer ring of a spinal disc tears or weakens, and the softer material inside pushes outward. Depending on how far it pushes and what it presses against, you get a bulge, a herniation, or — if the material breaks through completely — an extrusion. All of these get called "slip disc" in everyday conversation. It is a misleading term, but it is also the term most people search for, so we will use it here alongside the more accurate ones.

Back pain, disc problems, and sciatica — what is the difference?

These three overlap so much that people use them interchangeably. They are not the same thing.

Lower back pain is a symptom, not a diagnosis. It can come from muscles, ligaments, joints, discs, nerves, or a combination. In roughly 85–90% of cases, imaging does not reveal a single clear structural cause. The medical term for this is "non-specific low back pain," which is frustrating to hear but important to understand — it means the problem is usually mechanical and functional rather than something that needs surgical repair.

A disc herniation (what people call a "slip disc") is a structural finding. The disc material pushes out and may press on a nerve root. But here is the part that surprises people: plenty of disc herniations cause no symptoms at all. MRI studies of people with zero back pain regularly show bulging or herniated discs. The herniation matters when it irritates a nerve — which brings us to sciatica.

Sciatica is pain that radiates down the leg, usually along the path of the sciatic nerve — from the lower back or buttock, down the back or side of the thigh, sometimes into the calf and foot. It happens when a nerve root in the lower spine is compressed or irritated. A herniated disc is the most common cause, but spinal stenosis, piriformis syndrome, and other conditions can produce the same pattern.

So: back pain is the broad category. A disc problem is one possible cause. Sciatica is one possible consequence of a disc problem — or of other things entirely. They can exist together or separately.

How common is this?

Very. A pooled analysis of Indian studies found a point prevalence of about 48% for low back pain — meaning that at any given time, nearly half the adult population has it. Lifetime prevalence is around 66%. Among IT professionals and desk workers in Indian cities, studies consistently report rates between 20% and 50%, depending on how the question is asked and over what time period. One Gadchiroli community study found that 12% of adults with back or neck pain had radiculopathy — the nerve-root involvement that underlies sciatica.

It is not a condition of old age. It hits working-age adults harder than any other group, and the economic cost — in lost workdays, reduced productivity, and medical expenses — is enormous.

When to worry, and when not to

Most back pain, even severe back pain, is not dangerous. It is painful and disabling, but it is not a sign that your spine is damaged beyond repair. The large majority of episodes improve within six to twelve weeks, and even disc herniations can shrink or resorb over time as the body's inflammatory response clears the displaced material.

There are exceptions. You should see a doctor promptly if you have:

  • Loss of bladder or bowel control alongside back pain
  • Progressive weakness in one or both legs
  • Numbness in the saddle area (inner thighs and groin)
  • Severe, unrelenting pain that does not change with position
  • Back pain following significant trauma
  • Unexplained weight loss alongside new back pain

These can indicate cauda equina syndrome, fracture, or other serious conditions that need urgent investigation. They are rare, but they are the reason a proper clinical assessment matters before anyone starts treating you.

The MRI trap

This deserves its own section because it is one of the biggest sources of unnecessary anxiety. People get an MRI, see the words "disc bulge" or "disc herniation" or "degenerative changes" on the report, and assume the worst. But these findings are extremely common in people with no pain at all.

By age 40, roughly 50% of people with no symptoms will have a disc bulge on MRI. By age 60, that number climbs higher. "Degenerative disc disease" sounds alarming, but it is essentially normal ageing of the spine — the same way grey hair is normal ageing of the scalp. It does not mean your spine is falling apart.

An MRI is valuable when there are red-flag symptoms, when surgery is being considered, or when symptoms are not responding to conservative care. Getting one too early, before giving the body time to recover, often does more harm than good — because it gives you a scary-sounding label for something that might have resolved on its own.

What does the evidence say about treatment?

The short version: for most people, conservative care works. Surgery is reserved for the minority who do not improve.

Physiotherapy and exercise

This is the backbone (no pun intended) of treatment for both non-specific low back pain and disc-related sciatica. Structured exercise — core stabilisation, flexibility work, progressive loading, and functional movement retraining — has the strongest evidence base of any intervention for chronic low back pain. A 2022 European Spine Journal systematic review on physiotherapy for sciatica found that it was more effective than minimal intervention for long-term pain, though the authors noted that many included trials had methodological limitations.

The key word is active. Bed rest makes things worse. Staying as active as pain allows, with guided exercise progression, is consistently better than rest, bracing, or passive treatments alone.

Manual therapy

Hands-on techniques — joint mobilisation, soft-tissue work, neural mobilisation — can help with pain relief and movement in the short term. They work best as part of a broader rehabilitation programme, not as a standalone treatment. If someone is doing only manual therapy and no exercise, the approach is incomplete.

Spinal decompression

Motorised spinal decompression applies controlled traction to the spine, aiming to reduce intradiscal pressure and encourage retraction of herniated material. Patients often report significant pain relief. The independent evidence is mixed — controlled trials have not consistently shown it to be better than simpler traction methods — but it remains a useful tool in a comprehensive programme, particularly for patients who respond poorly to other conservative options.

Shockwave therapy

Extracorporeal shockwave therapy delivers focused acoustic pulses to the affected area. It is better established for tendinopathies (like plantar fasciitis and tennis elbow), but emerging evidence suggests a role in chronic lower back pain, particularly where myofascial trigger points are involved. It is not a first-line treatment for disc herniation, but it can complement a rehabilitation programme for persistent muscular pain around the spine.

Class IV laser therapy

High-intensity laser therapy delivers photonic energy to deep tissues, aiming to reduce inflammation, promote tissue repair, and relieve pain. The evidence for its use in back pain is still developing — early studies are promising for pain reduction, but larger, well-controlled trials are needed. We use it selectively for patients with inflammatory components to their pain.

TECAR therapy

TECAR (Transfer of Energy Capacitive and Resistive) uses radiofrequency energy to generate deep tissue heating. In capacitive mode it targets water-rich tissues like muscles; in resistive mode it reaches deeper structures — joints, tendons, and the tissues around the spine. For back pain, it is used to reduce muscle spasm, improve local blood flow, and accelerate tissue healing. It works best as a preparatory or complementary modality — loosening tissues before exercise or manual therapy, rather than as a standalone treatment.

Aquatic rehabilitation

A 2022 meta-analysis of 13 trials found that aquatic therapy reduced pain intensity and lowered disability compared to control groups for chronic low back pain. The buoyancy of water unloads the spine, allowing movement and exercise that would be too painful on land. For patients who cannot tolerate land-based exercise — particularly in the early stages of a disc herniation or post-surgical recovery — this can be the bridge that gets them moving again.

Surgery

For disc herniation with persistent sciatica that has not responded to 6–12 weeks of conservative care, surgery (typically microdiscectomy) has good outcomes — a recent meta-analysis showed improved return-to-work rates compared to continued conservative management. But the key phrase is "has not responded." Surgery is not the first step, and for the majority of patients, it never becomes necessary.

For degenerative lumbar conditions without clear nerve compression, a separate meta-analysis found that intensive rehabilitation led to better functional mobility outcomes than spinal fusion surgery. The evidence, in other words, favours trying rehab properly before considering fusion.

What we do at Navthera

We see a lot of back pain and sciatica. It is, by a wide margin, the most common reason people walk through our doors. Our approach is structured, progressive, and built around what the evidence actually supports.

Assessment first

Every patient gets a thorough clinical assessment before any treatment begins. We need to understand whether the pain is discogenic, facet-related, muscular, neural, or a combination. We look at posture, movement patterns, neural tension, core stability, and functional capacity. The diagnosis drives the treatment plan — there is no one-size-fits-all protocol for "back pain."

Active rehabilitation

The core of what we do. Graded exercise progression — starting where the patient can start, not where we think they should be. Core stabilisation, spinal mobility, hip and thoracic flexibility, neural mobilisation, progressive loading, and functional retraining. The goal is not just to relieve pain but to build a spine that is resilient enough to handle the demands of daily life and work.

Robotic spinal decompression

For patients with disc herniations and nerve compression, we use motorised spinal decompression as part of the programme. It is not a replacement for active rehab — it is a complement. Decompression sessions are paired with strengthening and stabilisation work so that the relief gained during decompression is maintained by the muscles and movement patterns the patient builds.

Shockwave and laser therapy

Used selectively, not routinely. Shockwave for persistent myofascial pain and trigger points around the spine. Class IV laser for inflammatory pain that is not responding to exercise and manual therapy alone. These are tools, not treatments — they support the rehabilitation process, they do not replace it.

Aquatic rehabilitation

Our therapy pool is particularly useful for patients in the early stages of disc-related pain, where land-based exercise is too painful. The buoyancy reduces spinal loading while allowing movement and strengthening. Patients graduate from pool-based work to land-based rehabilitation as their pain allows.

TECAR therapy

Deep radiofrequency heating to reduce muscle spasm, improve circulation, and prepare tissues before exercise and manual therapy. Particularly useful for patients with chronic muscular tightness and guarding around the lumbar spine.

EMG biofeedback

Real-time monitoring of muscle activation during exercises. For back pain patients, this is especially useful for core stabilisation retraining — many people with chronic low back pain have lost the ability to properly recruit their deep stabilising muscles. EMG biofeedback shows them exactly when those muscles are firing and when they are not, making the exercise programme far more targeted.

Manual therapy and pain management

Joint mobilisation, soft-tissue techniques, dry needling, neural mobilisation, and electrotherapy — used to manage pain and restore movement in the early stages, always alongside an active exercise programme.

What recovery actually looks like

This is the part people most want to know, and the honest answer is: it depends. Non-specific low back pain often improves significantly within four to six weeks with the right management. Disc herniations with sciatica can take longer — three to six months is a realistic timeframe for most people, with the most dramatic improvements usually happening in the first six to eight weeks.

Recovery is rarely linear. There will be good days and bad days, and flare-ups do not mean you are back to square one. They usually mean you did a bit too much, or slept in an awkward position, or sat for too long. The overall trajectory matters more than any single day.

The patients who do best are the ones who stay consistent with their exercise programme, understand that some discomfort during rehab is normal (pain is not the same as harm), and make the lifestyle adjustments — sitting habits, workstation setup, movement breaks — that prevent the problem from recurring.
