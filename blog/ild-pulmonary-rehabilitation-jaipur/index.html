Interstitial lung disease (ILD) rehabilitation in Jaipur: breathing better is trainable

The diagnosis usually arrives after months of worsening breathlessness. By the time a CT scan shows the scarring and a pulmonologist puts a name to it — ILD, pulmonary fibrosis, hypersensitivity pneumonitis — most patients have already stopped doing things they used to do without thinking. Walking to the market. Climbing one flight of stairs. Playing with their grandchildren.

Cardio-Respiratory Rehabilitation

The scarring in interstitial lung disease is, for now, irreversible. No medication undoes it. No therapy erases it. But here is something most patients are never told clearly enough: how much your lungs can do and how much you can do with the lungs you have are two different things. Rehabilitation works on the second. And the gap between untrained and trained is often the difference between being housebound and living a reasonable life.

What ILD actually does to the body

Interstitial lung disease is not one disease. It is a group of over 200 conditions that cause inflammation and scarring (fibrosis) of the lung tissue. The most common forms in India are hypersensitivity pneumonitis, sarcoidosis, idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF), and connective tissue disease-related ILD. Indian data from the PGIMER registry suggest a prevalence of roughly 50–100 per 100,000 population, with hypersensitivity pneumonitis and sarcoidosis making up a larger share than in Western countries.

The scarring thickens the walls of the air sacs (alveoli), making it harder for oxygen to pass into the blood. The lungs become stiff. Breathing becomes work — physical work that uses energy, tires the muscles, and limits everything downstream.

But here is what matters for rehabilitation: the limitation most patients feel is not purely a lung problem. It is a whole-body problem. The heart works harder to compensate. The skeletal muscles decondition because the patient stops moving. The breathing muscles fatigue because they are overloaded. Anxiety about breathlessness creates a cycle where the patient avoids activity, loses fitness, and becomes breathless at even lower levels of exertion. Each part of this chain is trainable.

What pulmonary rehabilitation is (and is not)

Pulmonary rehabilitation is not breathing exercises done at home from a YouTube video. It is a structured, supervised programme that combines exercise training, breathing retraining, education, and psychological support. It is delivered by physiotherapists who understand respiratory physiology and who can titrate exercise intensity safely in a population where oxygen levels can drop unpredictably during exertion.

The core of it is exercise — progressive, monitored exercise that pushes the body just enough to trigger adaptation without crossing into dangerous desaturation. This is the part that most patients never receive, because most clinics do not have the monitoring equipment, the trained staff, or the structured programme to deliver it.

What rehabilitation does not do: it does not cure the disease. It does not reverse the fibrosis. It does not replace the medications the pulmonologist has prescribed. What it does is change the body's response to the disease — building the cardiovascular system, the muscles, and the breathing mechanics so that the same scarred lungs can support more activity, with less distress.

What the evidence says

The evidence for pulmonary rehabilitation in ILD is not as deep as it is for COPD, where decades of large trials have made rehabilitation a standard part of care. But it is growing, and the direction is consistent.

A 2018 randomised controlled trial found that pulmonary rehabilitation significantly improved exercise capacity (measured by the six-minute walk test), dyspnoea, and quality of life in ILD patients, with benefits persisting at six months. A 2025 pooled analysis of two randomised trials, published in Chest, found that rehabilitation was associated with improved medium-term survival in ILD — a finding the authors describe cautiously, noting that the mechanism may go beyond improved fitness.

A 2025 systematic review and meta-analysis examined which programme characteristics drive the best outcomes. The consistent finding across studies is that supervised, centre-based programmes with progressive exercise training produce the largest gains in walking distance and quality of life. Home-based programmes help, but the effect is smaller.

The evidence is not perfect. Sample sizes are small. The ILD umbrella covers diseases that behave very differently. But the clinical direction is clear enough that international guidelines now recommend pulmonary rehabilitation for ILD patients, not just COPD.

Why ILD rehabilitation is different from COPD rehabilitation

Many respiratory rehabilitation programmes are designed around COPD. They work, but ILD is a different beast, and the programme has to reflect that.

Desaturation is more unpredictable. COPD patients desaturate gradually and predictably during exercise. ILD patients can drop their oxygen levels suddenly and steeply. This means continuous pulse oximetry monitoring during exercise is not optional — it is essential. The therapist needs to adjust intensity in real time, not prescribe a fixed protocol and hope for the best.

The breathing pattern is different. COPD patients breathe slowly and trap air. ILD patients breathe rapidly and shallowly, because their stiff lungs cannot expand fully. The breathing retraining techniques are different: COPD uses pursed-lip breathing to slow expiration; ILD uses diaphragmatic training to deepen inspiration and slow the respiratory rate without increasing the work of breathing.

Fatigue is a bigger problem. ILD patients describe a bone-deep fatigue that goes beyond breathlessness. The scarred lungs work harder, the heart works harder, and the respiratory muscles consume a larger fraction of total energy. Exercise prescription has to account for this — shorter intervals, more rest, and careful attention to recovery between sessions.

The disease may be progressive. Some forms of ILD stabilise or respond to treatment. Others, particularly IPF, are progressive. Rehabilitation in a progressive disease is not about returning to a previous baseline — it is about maintaining the best possible function for as long as possible, and adapting the programme as the disease changes. This requires ongoing reassessment and flexibility that a fixed eight-week programme cannot provide.

Aquatic rehabilitation: why it matters for ILD

Water does something for respiratory patients that land-based exercise cannot. When you stand in a pool with water at chest level, the hydrostatic pressure compresses the chest wall and abdomen. This may sound counterintuitive — why add external pressure to lungs that already struggle? — but the effect is beneficial. The pressure assists exhalation, reduces the work of breathing at rest, and creates a training stimulus for the inspiratory muscles: they have to work slightly harder against the water pressure to breathe in, which strengthens them over time.

There is a second benefit that matters enormously for deconditioned patients: buoyancy. In the pool, a patient who can barely walk 50 metres on land can exercise for 20 or 30 minutes at a meaningful intensity because the water supports their body weight. The cardiovascular training effect is real, but the joint stress, the fall risk, and the energy cost of simply staying upright are dramatically reduced.

A Cochrane review of water-based exercise in COPD found comparable improvements in exercise capacity and quality of life to land-based training, with the added advantage that patients with joint problems, obesity, or balance issues — common comorbidities — could exercise at higher intensities than they could manage on land. The ILD-specific evidence in water is still thin (the disease is rarer, and the studies have not been done), but the physiological principles are the same, and clinical experience supports using it.

Our pool has warm water, which further reduces muscle tension and makes breathing feel easier. For patients who are anxious about exercising — and many ILD patients are, because every episode of breathlessness feels frightening — the pool is often where they first discover that they can push themselves without disaster.

What we do at Navthera for ILD patients

Pulmonary rehabilitation for ILD is not a generic gym programme with a pulse oximeter strapped on. It requires specific expertise, specific monitoring, and a structured progression. Here is what ours involves.

Detailed respiratory assessment

Baseline oxygen saturation at rest and during exertion. Six-minute walk test with continuous oximetry. Dyspnoea scoring. Functional capacity assessment. Breathing pattern analysis. We need to know exactly where the patient stands before we start — not just the lung function numbers from the pulmonologist (which tell us about the disease), but the functional numbers (which tell us about the person).

Progressive aerobic conditioning

The centrepiece of the programme. Exercise intensity is prescribed based on the walk test and titrated using real-time oxygen monitoring. We start conservatively and progress gradually. The goal is to build cardiovascular endurance and peripheral muscle strength without crossing the desaturation threshold that makes exercise unsafe. For many patients, this starts with interval training — short bursts of walking or cycling with rest periods — and progresses to longer continuous exercise as tolerance improves.

Breathing retraining

Diaphragmatic breathing to improve ventilatory efficiency. Pacing and energy conservation techniques for daily activities. Controlled breathing strategies for exertion — climbing stairs, carrying groceries, walking uphill. These are not abstract exercises. They are practical skills that the patient uses every day, and we practise them in the context of real-world tasks, not just lying on a treatment table.

Inspiratory muscle training

The breathing muscles in ILD are overloaded. They work harder than normal with every breath. Targeted inspiratory muscle training strengthens them so that the same breath requires less effort, leaving more reserve for activity. This is done with calibrated resistance devices, progressively loaded over weeks.

Aquatic rehabilitation

Pool-based exercise as described above. Warm-water walking, interval training, chest-level immersion for inspiratory muscle loading. The pool sessions complement the land-based programme — they allow higher-intensity cardiovascular training with lower perceived exertion, reduce anxiety about breathlessness, and improve overall exercise tolerance. Our pool has a patient lift for safe entry and exit for patients with limited mobility or balance issues.

Strength training

Peripheral muscle weakness is both a consequence and a driver of disability in ILD. The patient stops moving, the muscles weaken, and weaker muscles need more oxygen per unit of work — which the scarred lungs cannot supply. Breaking this cycle requires targeted resistance training for the major muscle groups: legs, core, upper body. Light weights, high repetitions, monitored throughout.

Education and self-management

Understanding energy conservation. Recognising warning signs. Knowing when to push and when to rest. Managing exacerbations. Using supplemental oxygen correctly if prescribed. Many ILD patients are on antifibrotic medications with side effects that affect appetite, energy, and mood — the programme has to accommodate all of this.

Ongoing reassessment

ILD is not static. The disease may progress, stabilise, or respond to treatment. The rehabilitation programme has to change with it. We reassess walking distance, oxygen saturation responses, strength, and functional capacity at regular intervals and adjust the programme accordingly. This is not an eight-week course with a graduation certificate. It is an ongoing process of adaptation.

What families should know

ILD rehabilitation is slow. The gains are real but incremental. A patient who could walk 150 metres at the start might walk 250 metres after three months. That does not sound dramatic on paper. But in practice, it is the difference between being unable to reach the corner shop and being able to walk there and back. It is the difference between needing to rest twice while getting dressed and doing it in one go.

Breathlessness during exercise is expected and, within monitored limits, safe. This is the hardest thing for families to accept. The instinct is to stop the patient from exerting themselves. But avoiding all exertion is what drives the deconditioning cycle that makes the disease feel worse than the lung damage alone would cause.

Consistency matters more than intensity. Three supervised sessions a week with home exercises on the other days is the standard structure. Missing weeks and trying to make up for it with intense sessions does not work — it increases the risk of desaturation events and discourages the patient.

And one thing that is rarely said: rehabilitation improves mood. The anxiety and depression that accompany ILD are not weakness. They are a predictable response to a disease that makes the most basic human activity — breathing — feel difficult and frightening. Structured exercise, social contact with the rehabilitation team, and the experience of measurable progress address this directly. Some patients tell us the psychological change is as important as the physical one.
