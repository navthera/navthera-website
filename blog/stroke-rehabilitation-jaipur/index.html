What stroke rehabilitation can do for you

Recovery does not stop at six months, practice is what drives it, and several treatments have real numbers behind them. Here is what stroke rehabilitation achieves — and how to make the most of it.

Stroke Recovery

Recovery after a stroke goes on far longer than most families are told, and much of what happens in that time is in your hands. Improvement continues well past six months, several treatments have measurable results behind them, and the thing that drives most of it — practice — is something a family can ask for and help with.

Recovery keeps going far longer than most families are told

Take one thing from this article: there is no six-month cliff.

Researchers followed 219 stroke survivors and measured how quickly they improved at each stage (Journal of Neurophysiology, 2019). Improvement ran at around 5.2 per cent a week early on, about 2.7 per cent a week between six and eighteen months, and about 1.4 per cent a week beyond eighteen months — smaller, but still measurable and still statistically significant. The curve flattens. It does not stop. The 2026 AHA/ASA guideline puts it plainly: recovery extends over months to years, with no clearly defined endpoint.

What can be gained late is not trivial either. At the Queen Square programme in London, 224 people a median of eighteen months past their stroke were given intensive upper-limb therapy, 90 hours over three weeks. Their Fugl-Meyer scores — the standard measure of arm and hand movement — rose from a median of 26 to 37, and kept rising over six months of follow-up (JNNP, 2019). It was an observational cohort without a control group, so it shows what is achievable rather than proving cause.

If you are reading this months or years after a stroke, that is the message to hold on to. It is not too late to begin.

Starting promptly gives everything else a head start

The 2026 AHA/ASA guideline recommends that rehabilitation begin as soon as the person is medically stable, ideally within 48 hours. Sitting up, moving and starting to practise is what gets the process under way.

The evidence also shows what those first days should look like. AVERT, the largest trial in this area (2,104 patients across 56 stroke units, Lancet, 2015), and its dose-response analysis (Neurology, 2016) found short, frequent sessions associated with the best outcomes — roughly 13 per cent higher odds of a favourable outcome for each additional session in a day. The guideline adds one practical limit: moderate-to-high-intensity exercise and task practice are not for the first 24 hours.

Little and often, guided by the treating team, is the shape to ask for at the start; intensity is built up afterwards.

Practice is the active ingredient, and more of it helps

Rehabilitation works through repetition: doing the movement you want back, many times over, with a therapist shaping it as you go. NICE recommends at least three hours of therapy a day across all the therapies together, at least five days a week — and if three hours is too much at first, still a minimum of five days a week (NG236, 2023).

One number turns a vague worry into a specific question. When researchers counted what a typical session contains, they recorded an average of 32 upper-limb repetitions and 357 steps (Archives of Physical Medicine and Rehabilitation, 2009), against the several hundred repetitions — of the order of 400 to 600 — and one to two thousand steps a day that laboratory work points to. A session is finite — much of it goes on assessment, set-up and rest — so the way to close that gap is to use the rest of the day.

So the most useful question is not "how many sessions?" but "how many repetitions did we manage today, and what should we practise before the next one?"

The treatments with the strongest evidence behind them

Task-specific repetitive practice

Practising the thing you want back — standing up from a chair, stepping, reaching for a cup — over and over. Across 33 trials it added about 35 metres to walking distance, on moderate-quality evidence (Cochrane, 2016). NICE recommends offering it, and it is the backbone of most good programmes.

Circuit class therapy

A small group rotates through a series of workstations, each a different task. It gives one of the strongest mobility results in the literature: about 61 metres more on a six-minute walk and 0.15 m/s faster walking, on moderate-quality evidence (Cochrane, 2017). Working alongside others doing the same thing is, for many, what keeps them coming back.

Fitness training

Across 75 trials, fitness training after stroke improved fitness, balance and walking, with no serious adverse events reported (Cochrane, 2020).

Mirror therapy

A mirror is positioned so the moving, stronger hand appears where the weaker one is. Cochrane (2018) found moderate-quality evidence of benefit for movement and everyday activities, and the 2026 AHA/ASA guideline supports it alongside conventional therapy. It needs almost no equipment, so it carries on easily at home.

Robotics and electrical stimulation

A robot can support a weak arm through far more repetitions than can be assisted by hand. Cochrane's 2018 review found high-certainty evidence that robot-assisted arm training improves daily activities and arm function; the gains are consistent, and modest in size. For walking, robotic gait training used together with conventional physiotherapy probably helps more people walk independently — about one extra independent walker for every nine treated — with most of that benefit in the first three months.

Functional electrical stimulation does something different: small electrical pulses make a muscle contract when the message from the brain is not yet getting through, so a movement can be practised before it can be produced voluntarily. It is used at the shoulder too, where stimulation reduces subluxation — the joint slipping partly out of place — in the short term.

Practice in water

Water carries much of your body weight, so standing and stepping can be practised before they are possible on land. Across 13 trials in 468 people with chronic stroke, aquatic therapy improved Berg Balance Scale scores by about 3.7 points (Neurology International, 2026) — a modest gain, alongside land-based practice rather than instead of it. When extra practice may count most

Timing may matter as well as quantity. The CPASS trial (PNAS, 2021) gave extra arm training at different points after stroke and found the largest improvement at one year among those treated at 60 to 90 days. It was an early-phase trial of 72 participants, so treat it as a promising signal rather than a rule — but inside the first few months it is a good reason to get organised now.

Spasticity and shoulder pain, and what is done about them

Spasticity — muscles that stiffen and resist being moved — affects about a quarter of stroke survivors, and close to 40 per cent of those left with weakness after a first stroke. The reassuring figure is the next one: around 9 per cent develop spasticity severe enough to be disabling (Frontiers in Neurology, 2021). It is watched for from the start and reviewed with your doctor, and it does not stop active practice — continuing to use the arm and the leg is part of the treatment.

Pain in the shoulder on the weaker side affects up to 47 per cent of survivors (ACNR, 2026), and it is worth mentioning the day it appears. How a heavy, weak arm is positioned and handled — in the chair, during transfers, in bed — is routine physiotherapy work, and where the shoulder has begun to drop out of the socket, electrical stimulation reduces that in the short term. A comfortable shoulder is a shoulder that gets used.

Why this article matters in India

Stroke is becoming more common here: incidence rose from 76 per 100,000 in 1990 to 88 per 100,000 in 2021 (GBD 2021). Services are expanding unevenly: a 2024 survey of 95 Indian hospitals found about half had a dedicated stroke unit, and a 2023 review counted some 150 designated rehabilitation facilities nationally.

The most striking finding is not about buildings. Asked why they had not used rehabilitation, stroke survivors in India named neither cost nor distance most often: 83.3 per cent simply did not know it was available to them.

Which is what makes an article like this worth passing on: if you know a family told there is nothing more to be done, the evidence above says otherwise.
