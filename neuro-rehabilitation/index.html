Neuro Rehabilitation

What neuro rehabilitation is, and who it helps

Neuro rehabilitation is physiotherapy for people whose difficulty with movement comes from the nervous system — the brain, the spinal cord, or the nerves that carry instructions to the muscles — rather than from a joint or a muscle alone. A stroke, a spinal injury, Parkinson's disease or a nerve condition can take away things that were once automatic: standing up, crossing a room, holding a cup steadily.

This is the department at Navthera in Jaipur that works on exactly that. The nervous system is able to relearn, and rehabilitation is the structured, repeated practice that helps it do so — safer movement, fuller use of the arms and legs, and as much daily independence as your condition allows.

How rehabilitation helps, by condition

Stroke and brain injury

After a stroke or head injury, one side of the body is often weak, and muscles can tighten and resist movement. Rehabilitation is structured, repeated practice of ordinary actions — sitting up, walking, using the affected arm — until the nervous system relearns them. FES uses electrical stimulation to activate muscles too weak to switch on by themselves, and virtual reality turns that repetition into tasks you can see and respond to.

Spinal cord injury and spinal conditions

When the spinal cord is injured, movement and sensation below that level are affected, and sitting balance, transfers and standing have to be relearned. Rehabilitation builds on the muscles that still respond: an active passive trainer moves the legs for someone who cannot yet move them and assists as effort returns, while the aquatherapy pool takes weight off the body so upright practice becomes possible. Bladder control is often affected too, and the FMS chair uses magnetic stimulation to activate and strengthen the pelvic floor while you stay fully clothed — work shared with pelvic health physiotherapy. Recovery after spinal surgery overlaps with orthopaedic rehabilitation.

Parkinson's, MS and progressive conditions

In Parkinson's disease, movements become smaller and slower, steps shorten and turning can bring hesitation or a sense of freezing; multiple sclerosis and other progressive conditions add weakness and fatigue. Rehabilitation does not change the underlying condition, but it works on deliberately large, well-cued movement, steady balance practice and pacing that respects your energy. I-Gait, a robotic gait training system, supports the legs so walking can be practised in a controlled, repeated way.

Nerve injuries and neuropathies

When a nerve is damaged — by diabetes, by pressure or injury, by Bell's palsy, or after Guillain-Barré syndrome — the muscles it supplies weaken and sensation is often dulled. Rehabilitation rebuilds those muscles gradually and teaches you to protect a limb that can no longer feel properly. EMG biofeedback shows your own muscle activity on a screen as it happens, which helps when you are learning to switch a quiet muscle back on.

Children and older adults

In children with cerebral palsy or developmental delay, rehabilitation works alongside growth: practising the movements a child is ready for, keeping muscles long and joints comfortable, and using play so the repetition is willing rather than forced. In older adults the concern is usually unsteadiness and falls, where the work is strength, confident walking and safe balance, overlapping with geriatric physiotherapy and balance and vestibular rehabilitation.
