Sports Rehabilitation

Sports physiotherapy, and what it is for

Sports physiotherapy is for people who were doing something active and got hurt doing it. Sometimes the injury is sudden: a knee that gives way in a turn, an ankle rolled on uneven ground. Sometimes it builds up quietly through training until it can no longer be ignored.

What makes this work different is where it has to finish. Settling the pain is only the start, because the tissue then has to tolerate sprinting, cutting, landing and throwing again. That is what this department at Navthera in Jaipur is for — competitive athletes, and equally the person who wants to be back on the badminton court without wondering whether it will happen again.

How rehabilitation helps, by condition

Knee and ligament injuries

A knee that gives way when you turn or land usually means a ligament or the meniscus is injured, and the thigh muscle above it stops firing properly. Rehabilitation settles swelling, gets the knee straightening fully, then rebuilds quadriceps strength and the hip control that steadies a landing. Cryotherapy, controlled cold, eases pain and swelling; FES uses electrical stimulation to wake a quadriceps that will not switch on. Reconstructed knees are managed alongside orthopaedic rehabilitation.

Ankle and foot injuries

Rolling an ankle stretches the ligaments on the outside, and the joint can keep feeling unsteady long after the swelling settles. Achilles and heel pain build up differently, through training load. Rehabilitation restores the movement the ankle has lost, strengthens the calf and the muscles that steady the joint, and retrains balance so the foot reacts on uneven ground. Shockwave uses pressure waves for long-standing tendon and heel problems; cryotherapy helps a freshly swollen ankle.

Shoulder and upper limb injuries

Overhead sport loads the rotator cuff, the muscles holding the shoulder centred, so serving and throwing start to hurt; a shoulder that has come out feels unsafe overhead. Tennis and golfer's elbow flare with gripping. Rehabilitation calms the pain, restores movement, then loads the cuff, shoulder blade and forearm until overhead and gripping work feel steady. LASER, a light-based therapy, is used for pain and tissue healing; surgically repaired shoulders are shared with orthopaedic rehabilitation.

Muscle and soft tissue injuries

Hamstring, calf, groin and quadriceps strains tear at speed, in sprinting, kicking or a sudden stretch, and they tend to recur when the muscle is never rebuilt at full length. Rehabilitation loads it deliberately, guided by what the muscle will tolerate, and takes it out to the lengths sprinting demands. TECAR, radiofrequency that warms tissue at depth to lift local blood flow, makes hands-on work and loading more comfortable.

Performance and returning to sport

While an injury is protected, strength, power and confidence drop on that side, and compensations outlast the pain. This part of rehabilitation closes that gap: strength matched limb to limb, landing, sprinting and changing direction under control. The aquatic treadmill in the aquatherapy pool lets buoyancy take load off the legs, so walking and running patterns are practised with less impact, and AquaFit keeps conditioning going. Readiness is judged by what the limb can do.
